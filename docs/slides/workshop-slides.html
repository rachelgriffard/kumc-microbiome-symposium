16S rRNA Microbiome Analysis

KUMC Microbiome Symposium

Introduction

What is the microbiome?

  • Trillions of bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi inhabiting the body
  • Each body site has a distinct community: gut, skin, oral, vaginal, lung
  • Roles in immune development, metabolism, and disease susceptibility
  • The gut microbiome is dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes

Variability: across people and over time

Between individuals

  • Driven by diet, genetics, geography, early-life exposures, medications
  • “Normal” is difficult to define — variation is large even among healthy adults

Within an individual

  • Day-to-day fluctuations from diet, travel, illness, stress
  • Antibiotics can reshape the community within days; recovery takes weeks to months
  • Longitudinal within-person variation is often as large as between-person variation

Note

Variability is not noise — understanding what drives it is the central question of the field.

Types of Microbiome Data

Choosing the right technology

  • Not all microbiome questions require the same data
  • Think of the microbiome as a city — different technologies give different views of that city
  • The question you want to answer should drive your technology choice
  • Each method captures a different layer of information about the same community

Targeted Amplicon Sequencing — The Census

Big Question: Who is present in this community, and in what proportion?

  • Targets a conserved marker gene (16S rRNA for bacteria/archaea; ITS for fungi)
  • Knocking on every door and asking for the last name — broad identity, not fine detail
  • Resolves to genus level in most cases; species-level calls are unreliable
  • Cannot directly infer function from taxonomy alone

Note

Good for: community composition, diversity comparisons, clinical screening studies

Shotgun Metagenomics — The Architectural Blueprints

Big Question: Who is present, and what is their potential function?

  • Sequences all DNA in the sample — not limited to one marker gene
  • Like reading the blueprints of every building in the city — you see what equipment each organism has
  • Recovers large portions of genomes; reveals gene content and metabolic potential
  • Higher resolution than amplicon sequencing; higher cost and computational demand

Note

Good for: antibiotic resistance genes, strain-level resolution, functional capacity

Metatranscriptomics — The Conversations

Big Question: What is the community actively doing right now?

  • Sequences RNA (gene expression), not DNA
  • Like recording all conversations in the room at a specific moment in time
  • Shows which genes are being expressed at the moment of sampling
  • Results are highly time-dependent — a 2 AM sample looks very different from a 3 PM sample

Note

Good for: active gene expression, response to treatment, time-sensitive biological questions

Metabolomics — The City Output

Big Question: What is the chemical fingerprint of what this community produces?

  • Measures small molecules (metabolites) in the sample
  • Like checking the air quality monitor and drainage water — you see the outputs, not the producers directly
  • Captures chemicals that are hard to attribute to specific organisms
  • Many metabolites remain chemically unidentified

Note

Good for: functional outputs, host-microbiome interaction signals, treatment response markers

What is 16S rRNA Sequencing?

The 16S rRNA gene

  • Present in virtually all bacteria and archaea — a universal marker
  • ~1,500 bp long with alternating conserved and hypervariable regions (V1–V9)
  • Conserved regions → primer binding sites
  • Variable regions → distinguish taxa (like a genetic barcode)
  • Short-read sequencing targets one or two variable regions at a time

Variable region selection

Region Primers Amplicon Notes
V4 515F / 806R ~250 bp Earth Microbiome Project standard
V3–V4 341F / 805R ~460 bp Most common in clinical studies
V4–V5 515F / 926R ~380–415 bp Better archaeal coverage
V1–V2 27F / 338R ~310 bp Better gram-positive resolution

Important

Mixing primer pairs across studies is not valid — do not combine datasets from different primer sets.

Amplicons

  • The PCR product spanning the targeted variable region is called an amplicon
  • After sequencing, identical (or near-identical) amplicons are grouped into ASVs or OTUs
  • These serve as the working units of diversity in all downstream analysis

Where Data Comes From

Sequencing

  • MiSeq 2×250 or 2×300 bp is standard for 16S amplicon work
  • Paired-end: forward (R1) and reverse (R2) reads overlap in the amplicon middle — overlap corrects errors
  • Samples are pooled onto a single flow cell; reads are stochastically sampled → uneven depth across samples
  • Check the run report: % clusters passing filter, Q30 score

Confounders and batch effects

  • Technical variation accumulates at every step: extraction kit, lysis method, PCR cycles, who runs it, when
  • When that variation is systematic (e.g., all cases in batch 1, all controls in batch 2) it becomes a batch effect indistinguishable from biology
  • Design principle: balance treatment groups across batches

Warning

A confounded study design cannot be rescued in analysis. Balance must be built in at the design stage.

Amplification and pooling

  • Primer design determines which taxa you can detect — no primer set is universal
  • PCR amplifies exponentially: relative abundance ≠ absolute abundance
  • Chimera formation increases with cycle number — use high-fidelity polymerase and minimize cycles
  • After amplification, samples are normalized and pooled; pipetting error here creates depth variation

Public sequencing data

Major repositories for 16S rRNA data:

  • SRA (NCBI Sequence Read Archive) — largest archive
  • ENA (European Nucleotide Archive) — mirrors SRA with API access
  • QIITA — microbiome-specific, rich metadata
  • EMP (Earth Microbiome Project) — standardized protocols across environments
  • HMP (Human Microbiome Project) — reference human body site data

Important

When using public data: match primer pair, platform, and sequencing depth to your study. Metadata quality varies widely.

What Data Looks Like

FASTQ files

Four lines per read:

@SampleID_read1
GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA...
+
IIIIIIIIIIIIHHHGGG...
  • Line 1: read identifier
  • Line 2: nucleotide sequence
  • Line 3: + separator
  • Line 4: Phred quality scores (ASCII-encoded)

Q30 = 1 error per 1,000 bases · Quality degrades toward the 3′ end — expected

QC reports

FastQC and QIIME2 demux summarize show:

  • Per-base quality score distribution across the read length
  • Per-sample read counts after demultiplexing
  • Adapter/primer content

What to look for:

  • The “elbow” where quality drops — this guides your truncation length
  • Outlier samples with dramatically fewer reads
  • >20% unassigned reads signals a demultiplexing problem

Preprocessing

Trimming

Primer trimming (Cutadapt)

  • Remove primer sequences before any analysis — they are not biological sequence
  • Untrimmed primers will corrupt error modeling in DADA2 and produce spurious ASVs

Truncation

  • Cut reads at the quality “elbow” (where median quality drops below Q25–30)
  • R1 and R2 must still overlap after truncation — typically need ≥20–30 bp overlap
  • overlap = R1 length + R2 length − amplicon length
  • Use the same truncation parameters for all samples in the study

Denoising and merging

DADA2 pipeline:

  1. Learn error rates — model how often each base is miscalled at each quality score
  2. Denoise — correct reads using the error model; identify unique true sequences (ASVs)
  3. Merge pairs — align R1 and R2 in the overlap; non-overlapping pairs are discarded
  4. Remove chimeras — filter PCR-hybrid sequences

ASVs vs. OTUs

  • OTUs cluster at 97% similarity — arbitrary, not reproducible across datasets
  • ASVs are exact sequences — reproducible, higher resolution, can resolve single-base differences
  • The field has moved to ASVs

Normalization

Raw read counts are not comparable across samples — sequencing depth varies.

Method How Tradeoff
Rarefaction Subsample all samples to the same depth Simple; discards data
Relative abundance Divide by total reads Simple; compositional
CLR Log-ratio to geometric mean Statistically sound; requires care with zeros
DESeq2 / CSS RNA-seq-derived normalization Preferred for differential abundance

Check alpha rarefaction curves before choosing a depth — curves should plateau.

Dimension Reduction

Taxonomy assignment

  • Each ASV is compared to a reference database (SILVA, Greengenes2, NCBI)
  • QIIME2 uses a trained Naïve Bayes classifier; returns deepest confident level + probability score
  • Species-level calls are unreliable from the V4 region — genus is the practical limit

Taxonomic hierarchy:

Domain → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

Note

“Unclassified” is a real biological result — do not discard these ASVs.

Phylogenetic tree

  • Built from aligned ASV sequences using an evolutionary model
  • Required for Faith’s PD (phylogenetic alpha diversity) and UniFrac (phylogenetic beta diversity)
  • A community spanning diverse phyla scores higher than one with the same number of closely related ASVs

Guilds

  • Ecologically or functionally relevant groupings of taxa
  • Allows community-level interpretation beyond taxonomy

Downstream Analysis

Alpha diversity: within-sample diversity

Metric What it captures
Observed features Raw ASV count — depth-sensitive
Shannon Richness + evenness
Simpson Evenness-weighted — less sensitive to rare taxa
Faith’s PD Evolutionary distinctness of the community
Chao1 Estimated true richness accounting for undetected rare taxa
  • Use non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis, Wilcoxon)
  • Control for technical confounders (sequencing depth, extraction batch) before interpreting biologically

Beta diversity: between-sample diversity

Metric Considers
Bray-Curtis Abundances; no phylogeny
Jaccard Presence/absence only
UniFrac (unweighted) Phylogeny; presence/absence
UniFrac (weighted) Phylogeny + abundances
Aitchison Compositionally aware (CLR space)

Ordination (PCoA, NMDS, PCA) reduces the distance matrix to 2–3D for visualization.

Warning

Ordination plots suggest patterns — they do not prove them. Test with PERMANOVA; check dispersion with PERMDISP.

Differential abundance

Because relative abundances are compositional (sum to 1), standard tests fail.

Recommended tools:

  • ANCOM-BC2 — log-ratio approach; near-zero false positive rate; preferred
  • DESeq2 — borrowed from RNA-seq; performs well in practice
  • ALDEx2 — robust but conservative
  • Maaslin2 — handles multivariable models and mixed effects

Always apply FDR correction (Benjamini-Hochberg). Report q-values, not just p-values.

Common traps

  • Relative abundance artifacts — killing one taxon inflates all others’ relative abundance
  • Confounders — diet, antibiotics, BMI, PPI use can dominate microbiome variation
  • Low n — microbiome studies are highly variable; <20 per group is underpowered
  • Correlation ≠ causation — 16S data alone cannot establish mechanism
  • Taxonomy gaps — “unclassified” may be real biology, not analytical failure
